    Section Lesson 1 - Review

    Subsection Exponent Rules

    Let \(a,m,n\) be some numbers
    🔗
    1. \(a^ma^n = a^{m+n}\)
      🔗
      The left hand side says to multiply \(a\) \(m\) times and then multipliy that by \(a\) \(n\) times. So, you multiply \(a\) a total of \(m+n\) times.
      🔗
      \(2^2\cdot 2^3 = 2\cdot 2\cdot 2\cdot 2\cdot 2 = 2^5\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\)
      🔗
      The left hand side says to multiply \(a^m\) \(n\) times. So, rule 1 gives the right hand side (since adding \(m\) \(n\) ties is the same as \(m\cdot n\)).
      🔗
      \((2^2)3 = 2^2\cdot 2^2\cdot 2^2 = 2^{2+2+2} = 2^{2\cdot 3}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}\)
      🔗
      This can either be thought of as notation or a consequence of rule 1 and the fact that \(a^0=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      By rule 1, \(a^{-n}a^n = a^0 = 1\text{,}\) so by dividing both sides by \(a^n\) we get rule 3.
      🔗
      \(2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Logarithm Rules

    Definition 1.5.

    Let \(a,b,c\) be some numbers. We say \(\log_b(a) = c\) if \(a = b^c\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We mostly work with the natural logarithm, i.e., in the case when \(b=e\) and denote \(\log_b(x)\) as \(\ln(x)\)
    🔗

    Example 1.6.

    Simplify \(e^{ln(5x)}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(5x\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.7.

    Simplify \(e^{10-ln(5)}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(2\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    From the exponent arithmetic rules, we can deduce some for logarithm arithmetic. Let \(x,y\) be some numbers:
    🔗
    1. \(\ln(x)+\ln(y) = \ln(xy)\)
      🔗
      From rule 1 for exponents,
      \begin{equation*} e^{\ln(x)+\ln(y)} = e^{\ln(x)}e^{\ln(y)} = xy \end{equation*}
      Taking the natural log of both sides gives the desired rule.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\ln(x)-\ln(y) = \ln(\frac{x}{y})\)
      🔗
      From rules 1 and 2 for exponents,
      \begin{equation*} e^{\ln(x)-\ln(y)} = \frac{e^{\ln(x)}}{e^{\ln(y)}} = \frac{x}{y} \end{equation*}
      Taking the natural log of both sides gives the desired rule.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\ln(x^y) = y\ln(x)\)
      🔗
      From rule 2 for exponents,
      \begin{equation*} e^{y\ln(x)} = (e^{\ln(x)})^y = x^y \end{equation*}
      Taking the natural log of both sides gives the desired rule.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.8.

    Write \(\ln(\frac{xy}{z})\) as the sum of \(\ln(x),\ln(y),\ln(z)\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\ln(x)+\ln(y)-\ln(z)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.9.

    Write \(\ln(\frac{x^2}{y\sqrt{z}})\) as the sum of \(\ln(x),\ln(y),\ln(z)\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(2\ln(x)-\ln(y)-\frac{1}{2}\ln(z)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Trig Identities

    1. \(\displaystyle \tan(x) = \frac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \cot(x) = \frac{1}{\tan(x)} = \frac{\cos(x)}{\sin(x)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \csc(x) = \frac{1}{\sin(x)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \sec(x) = \frac{1}{\cos(x)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle \cos^2(x)+\sin^2(x) = 1\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Note, this is not an extensive list of trig identities, just some basic and common ones. I’d suggest finding a list online to have while doing homework problems. I’d also recommend brushing up in your unit circle
    🔗

    Example 1.10.

    Given \(\cos(\theta) = \frac{2}{3}\) and \(\theta\) is in the fourth quadrant, find \(\sin\theta,\cot\theta,\sec\theta\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\sin\theta = -\frac{\sqrt{5}}{3}\)
    🔗
    \(\cot\theta = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\)
    🔗
    \(\sin\theta = \frac{3}{2}\)
